Case Notes

Six clues that explain the strange spellings in this week’s words. Each one comes from where the word was born.

1 · Syllables are beats

A syllable is one beat of a word. Every syllable has one vowel sound. Tapping the beats helps you spell a long word in chunks.

Remember: One beat = one vowel sound = one chunk to spell.

2 · rh — a Greek spelling

rh comes from Greek words like rhythm, rhino and rhubarb. The h is silent — you only hear /r/. Rhyme borrowed its rh from rhythm about 400 years ago.

Remember: Rhythm’s rh is Greek, and the h is silent. Rhyme borrowed that rh.

3 · y can be a vowel

In Greek words, y often does the job of a vowel. In rhythm it says short /i/. Rhyme borrowed its y from rhythm about 400 years ago — there it says long /ī/, like time.

Remember: Rhythm’s y is the Greek vowel. Rhyme copied that y.

4 · ph says /f/

In words from Greek, p and h team up to say /f/. Metaphor comes from Greek meta (across) + pherein (to carry) — a metaphor carries meaning across.

Remember: Hear /f/ in metaphor? Write ph.

5 · The double l in alliteration

From Latin ad (to) + littera (letter). The d changed into an l to match, so we write all. Littera also gives us literacy and letter.

Remember: Alliteration = the same starting sound, close together. (Its name comes from the Latin for “letter”.)

6 · Words from around the world

English borrows words, and they often keep their old spelling. Stanza is Italian for “a room”. Ballad’s double l comes from French ballade and Latin ballare (“to dance”), like ballet.

Remember: An odd spelling is often a clue to where the word came from.
