Case Notes

Eight notes for your detective notebook. One idea per card. Come back to these any time you get stuck.

1 · Poetry and the stanza

Poetry is writing that plays with sound, beat and image. A stanza is a group of lines in a poem, with a gap before the next group.

Remember: A stanza is to a poem what a paragraph is to a story.

2 · The ballad

A ballad is a poem that tells a story, usually with a strong beat so it can be read aloud or sung. Banjo Paterson wrote famous bush ballads.

’Twas Mulga Bill, from Eaglehawk, that caught the cycling craze;Banjo Paterson, Mulga Bill’s Bicycle (1896)

Remember: A ballad is a story poem with a strong beat, often sung.

3 · Rhythm and rhyme

Rhythm is the beat you can clap along to. Rhyme is when word endings sound the same — camp and stamp in the diagram.

Once a jolly swagman camped by a billabong,
Under the shade of a coolibah tree,Banjo Paterson, Waltzing Matilda (popular version, 1903). Clap the beat.

Remember: Rhythm = the beat. Rhyme = matching end sounds.

4 · Simile

A simile compares two different things using the word like or as. The horse ran like the wind.

Remember: Simile = similar, using like or as.

5 · Metaphor

A metaphor says one thing is another thing. Our classroom was a beehive. It isn’t really a beehive — but it helps us picture a busy, buzzing room.

Remember: Metaphor says it is. Simile says it’s like.

6 · Alliteration

Alliteration repeats the same starting sound in words that are close together. It makes a line fun to say.

Remember: Alliteration = the same first sound, close together.

7 · A poet’s voice

Poets sometimes spell words the way people say them, to capture a voice. Paterson wrote ’twas for “it was”. When we read Dan Davies’ Moonthanguddi and Mookari, listen for how he captures a voice too. Aboriginal English is a real dialect — that’s a choice, not a mistake.

Remember: A poet can choose a voice. In our own formal writing, we use standard Australian spelling.

8 · Word treasure (just for reading)

swagman
a traveller who carried everything he owned in a rolled-up bundle (a swag)
billabong
a waterhole left behind when a river changes course
jumbuck
a sheep
coolibah
a kind of eucalypt tree that grows near water
squatter
a landholder who ran a large sheep or cattle station
Remember: You don’t need to spell these — just enjoy them when you read Paterson.

Explain It

Write two sentences explaining the difference between a simile and a metaphor. Give an example of each. Check your spelling against the Spelling Spy.

Sentence starters

• A simile compares two things using …
• For example, …
• A metaphor is different because it says …
• For example, …

Cloze — fill the gaps:

A ______ compares two things using “like” or “as”, such as “as fast as a ______”. A ______ says one thing ______ another, such as “the ______ was a golden coin”.

Tap a word to drop it into your writing box.

🔒 Write at least one sentence of your own first — then the worked example unlocks.
Worked example (one way to do it): A simile compares two things using “like” or “as”, such as “the dog ran like a rocket”. A metaphor says one thing is another, such as “the dog was a rocket”. Compare it with yours. Did you explain the difference, give two examples, and spell simile and metaphor correctly?

Detective challenge (extension)

Add one more sentence using an extension word: onomatopoeia (a word that sounds like its noise — sizzle, crash), personification (giving human actions to non-human things — the wind whispered) or colloquial (everyday, chatty language — g’day, arvo).
